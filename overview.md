We played around with Polycam and lasers, measuring pieces of tomb stones. I have experience with Polycam but I do not with the laser we used in class. I prefer using Polycam over the laser because of the user-friendliness of the application and I enjoy the fact that it is as accessible as downloading an app and not purchasing an expensive laser device. 
The best trick when using the Polycam application is to move slowly to allow for several photos to be taken - the more the merrier in this case really pays off. When walking in the cemetery, I took two different Polycams of a tombstone, (uploaded in this repository- hopefully both are viewable) and one of them was significantly more distorted than the other because I was moving faster and I stumbled once. The second go was much better and the image created was significantly less distorted. 
Having access to these devices allows for more ability to record archaeological finds. I could never do an artist interpretation/rendering of the tombstones with the speed and accuracy that the polycam or lasers provide.

Ethics in digital archaeology 
- Because digitalization of the archaeological process allows the discipline to be more accessible
- I resonated with this passage from the reading: The result of this mismatch in ethics and practice is the creation of archaeologists who utilize digital forms, but whose archaeology is ungrounded in frameworks that specifically consider the ethical burdens of digital tools, methodology, and theory.
- Duty of care in archaeology should always be at the forefront of our archaeological minds, either digitally or not. We may need to adopt more stringent ethical guidelines with the adoption of the digital, and use of AI for example. That is a non-negotiable discipline wide standard
- Aspirational code of ethics: intentionally general and meant to apply to majority of archaeological projects. BUT when there is a failure to meet an organization's code of ethics, there aren't many if any consequences under this system because it is assumed that these violations will be handled more formally by universities and organizations.
- prescriptive code of ethics: System of rules that specifically lists the acceptable and unacceptable behaviours within the discipline. The RPA (register of professional archaeologists) is purposefully prescriptive - listing what archaeologist shall and shall not do. Enforced via grievance process
- The issue then is how does Digital archaeology fit into all of this? since the field isnt provided as aspirational or as prescriptive, placing the onus on the individual to dictate their professional behaviour
- ethical burden on the weight an archaeologist must give to whether any given thig is ethically appropriate or inappropriate.
- IMO the discipline needs a bit of both codes of ethics, we cannot expect the archaeologist to all function the same way with the same personal ethics and reflexive behaviour. I think we need significantly more standards.
  - Producing knowledge to be shared - no barriers around blackbox technologies. Even in excavation archaeology, we should be trying to keep as much information as accessible as possible. Same should apply in digital archaeology
  - Digital tools may add new ethical burdens, but I believe we need to harness these tools in our archaeological practices because it can help preserve files in the long term, as well as create excavation techniques that are less invasive and therefore less damaging to the site.
  - preparing for the reality of the digitally mediated archaeology that is modern practice: digital archaeology is not going anywhere, we need to tighten up our bootstraps. More discussions need to be had to standardize the digital methodologies in the discipline
  - We may have adopted digital tools too quickly, there needs to be a focus on the adoption of discipline wide standards related to archaeological ethics. They are mismatched.
  - to make change, everyone who practices needs to consider their ethical burdens, Duty of care is always at the centre of everything we do in archaeology. Digital or not.
